Limitation of Liability High risk
They can only pay you back 30 days of fees — no matter how big the damage
Liability cap §8.2 · Page 7
What this means
If the provider's software causes you a massive data breach, loses all your business records, or goes offline for weeks — the maximum they'll ever pay you back is one month's subscription fee. For a ₹5,000/month SaaS tool, that's ₹5,000. Even if you lose ₹50 lakh worth of business. This is extremely one-sided — industry standard is 12 months of fees.
Exact clause text
"Provider's aggregate liability shall not exceed the total fees paid by Customer in the thirty (30) day period immediately preceding the claim giving rise to liability."
Ask for this instead: "Provider's aggregate liability shall not exceed the total fees paid by Customer in the twelve (12) month period preceding the claim."

This changes the cap from 30 days to 12 months — a 12× improvement. Most vendors will accept this without pushback. If they refuse, ask for a minimum floor of ₹50,000 regardless of subscription amount.
Unilateral amendment rights High risk
They can change any rule — including pricing — with just 7 days notice
Amendment rights §12.1 · Page 11
What this means
The provider can change literally any part of the contract — prices, data usage rules, features, cancellation terms — and all they have to do is send you an email 7 days before. If you keep using the service after those 7 days, you've automatically agreed to the new terms. You have no actual say.
Exact clause text
"Provider reserves the right to modify these Terms at any time. Provider will notify Customer of changes via email with a minimum of seven (7) days notice. Continued use of the Service constitutes acceptance of the modified Terms."
Minimum ask: Change 7 days to 30 days notice, and add "Material changes require Customer's written consent."

Better ask: "Price changes shall not apply to the current contract term and shall only take effect upon renewal." This locks in your price for the contract duration.
Broad IP assignment High risk
Everything you build using their platform belongs to them
IP ownership §6.1 · Page 5
What this means
Any reports, analyses, documents, or outputs you generate using their platform automatically become their property. If you use this tool to write your company's strategy document or financial projections, they technically own it. This is unusually broad — most SaaS tools only claim ownership of the platform itself, not what you produce with it.
Exact clause text
"Customer acknowledges that all work product, outputs, analyses, and derivative works created using the Platform shall be the sole and exclusive property of Provider."
This clause should be deleted entirely or replaced with: "Customer retains full ownership of all data, documents, and outputs generated by Customer using the Platform."

This is a non-negotiable red flag. If the vendor won't change it, walk away.
Auto-renewal Medium risk
Renews for 12 months automatically unless you cancel 60 days before
Auto-renewal §4.3 · Page 3
What this means
The contract locks you in for another full year automatically. The 60-day cancellation window is longer than the typical 30-day standard — meaning you need to decide about renewal two months before it ends, which is easy to miss. If you miss it, you're paying for another year whether you want to or not.
Exact clause text
"Unless Customer provides written notice of non-renewal at least sixty (60) days prior to the end of the then-current subscription term, this Agreement shall automatically renew for successive twelve (12) month periods."
Ask to reduce to 30 days: "Unless Customer provides written notice of non-renewal at least thirty (30) days prior to the end of the then-current term..."

Also ask the vendor to send you a renewal reminder email 90 days before expiry. Many will agree to this as a goodwill gesture. Set a calendar reminder for yourself regardless.
Governing law Review
Any dispute must be resolved in Delaware courts, USA
Jurisdiction §15.1 · Page 13
What this means
If you ever have a legal dispute with this vendor, you'd need to file in a Delaware court in the United States. If you're based in India, that means international litigation — which is extremely expensive and impractical for most businesses. This is standard for US companies but worth knowing.
Exact clause text
"This Agreement shall be governed by the laws of the State of Delaware, United States, without regard to its conflict of law provisions."
If you're India-based: Ask to add "or the courts of [your city], India, at Customer's election" — giving you choice of jurisdiction.

Alternatively, ask for international arbitration (e.g. ICC or SIAC) instead of court proceedings. Most global SaaS vendors will accept this as it protects them too.
